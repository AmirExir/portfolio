 1.⁠ ⁠Can you walk us through the ERCOT transmission planning process, from identifying a system need to evaluating alternatives and obtaining approval for a proposed transmission project?
Suggested answer
I would start by defining the system need, the evidence behind it, and the date by which it must be resolved. The driver could be load growth, generation changes, a reliability deficiency, or congestion. ERCOT develops its Regional Transmission Plan annually for the upcoming six years, while longer-term assessments help identify investments that remain useful across different futures. I would coordinate early with the affected transmission service providers, or TSPs, to establish a consistent study basis.
Next, I would validate the base cases and document load, dispatch, topology, equipment ratings, and planned facilities. I would use the required contingency analyses and appropriate stability and short-circuit studies to demonstrate the problem. Alternatives could include reconductoring, new circuits, transformer additions, reactive support, or permissible operational measures. I would compare technical performance, cost, constructability, outage requirements, and future flexibility. Reliability needs and economic benefits have distinct justification paths; I would apply the relevant ERCOT criteria to each.
The project submission would document the need, alternatives, study evidence, estimated cost, and achievable in-service date for Regional Planning Group review. The required review depends on the project tier. Under the standard process, Tier 1 involves ERCOT independent review, Technical Advisory Committee review and comment, and Board endorsement. Tier 2 generally receives endorsement through ERCOT's independent-review recommendation, while Tier 3 receives RPG acceptance through successful comment resolution. Inclusion in the regional plan alone is not project endorsement.
Where a Certificate of Convenience and Necessity is required, the responsible TSP pursues PUCT certification and the applicable routing approval. ERCOT's recommendation supports the need determination; PUCT evaluates the statutory certification criteria. I would integrate that approval path with right-of-way, detailed engineering, procurement, construction outages, and commissioning. My responsibility would be to maintain a defensible connection between the demonstrated need, selected investment, approvals, and delivery date.

 2.⁠ ⁠Suppose an ERCOT transmission corridor experiences significant thermal overloads and voltage violations under N-1 contingencies. How would you investigate the problem and develop technically and economically viable mitigation solutions?
Suggested answer
I would first reproduce the violations in an agreed ERCOT planning case and confirm that they represent a physical problem. I would check the affected facilities, outage definitions, normal and emergency ratings, voltage criteria, dispatch, transformer taps, reactive limits, and modeled upgrades. I would separate pre-existing violations from those introduced by the study scenario. Nonconvergence would remain an unresolved outcome until I determined whether it reflected numerical settings, islanding, or genuine voltage instability.
Next, I would identify the mechanism. For thermal overloads, I would examine post-contingency MW and Mvar flows, limiting equipment, transfer distribution factors, and how the outage redirects power. For voltage issues, I would examine reactive reserves, generator capability limits, transformer controls, and the location of reactive losses. P–V and Q–V analysis would help quantify transfer and reactive margins; dynamic simulation would be necessary if recovery after a fault or control behavior could be limiting. Linear sensitivities would guide screening, with final conclusions confirmed using AC analysis.
I would develop a short list of solutions matched to those mechanisms: reconductoring, additional circuits or transformers, topology changes, and appropriately located capacitors, reactors, SVCs, STATCOMs, or synchronous condensers. I would also evaluate allowable redispatch, switching, or other operating measures as potential interim solutions. Reactive compensation may improve voltage and reduce reactive current, but I would verify whether it actually resolves the thermal constraint. Any operational mitigation would need acceptable timing, enforceability, and consistency with applicable criteria.
Finally, I would compare compliant alternatives on lifecycle cost, congestion relief, losses, construction outages, permitting, equipment lead times, and robustness to future growth. I would rerun the full relevant contingency set to detect transferred problems, check stability and fault-duty impacts, and document the residual risks. My recommendation would identify both the preferred permanent project and any validated interim measures, with a clear link between the limiting contingency, the mitigation mechanism, and the required in-service date.

 3.⁠ ⁠How do NERC TPL, FAC, and MOD Reliability Standards influence transmission planning studies, and how would you ensure compliance with the applicable requirements?
Suggested answer
I view these standards as complementary controls: TPL establishes required system performance, FAC addresses facility connections and ratings, and MOD establishes the data and models needed to make the studies credible. I would first build an applicability matrix identifying our registered functions, the responsible Planning Coordinator and Transmission Planner, and the versions and implementation dates that apply. Approval of a revised standard does not necessarily mean every requirement is already enforceable.
TPL-001 drives the planning assessment: appropriate study horizons and sensitivities, normal conditions, applicable contingency categories, and steady-state, stability, and short-circuit performance. I would document voltage and stability criteria, credible event definitions, and allowable system adjustments. Where performance is deficient, I would develop and track a corrective action plan, including the required completion date and any permitted interim measures. I would treat extreme-event assessments according to their specific requirements rather than assume every event has the same pass/fail rules. I would also map applicable TPL-008 extreme-temperature planning obligations to their implementation milestones.
For FAC, I would focus on FAC-001 interconnection requirements, FAC-002 coordinated interconnection studies, and FAC-008 facility ratings. Ratings must reflect the limiting applicable equipment, including terminal equipment, rather than only the conductor rating. I would confirm normal and emergency rating assumptions and ensure interconnection impacts are coordinated with affected entities. The Transmission Planner and Planning Coordinator have assessment responsibilities, while owners and other applicable parties have defined data and coordination obligations; outsourcing a study does not eliminate those responsibilities.
For MOD, I would use MOD-032 for modeling-data requirements and submission, MOD-033 for system-model validation, and applicable generator capability and dynamic-model verification requirements, including MOD-025 and MOD-026-2. For MOD-026-2, I would follow the phased compliance dates and retain relevant verification history from predecessor MOD-026-1 and MOD-027-1. I would reconcile equipment data, control settings, load representation, and validated models before accepting conclusions. My compliance package would retain the governing requirements, model versions, study scripts, results, review records, data requests, and corrective-action status. I would assign an accountable owner to each requirement and independently review the evidence. A clean contingency report alone would not establish compliance or engineering correctness.

 4.⁠ ⁠A developer proposes connecting a 1,000 MW solar-plus-storage facility to the ERCOT transmission system. What technical studies would you perform, and how would you determine whether network upgrades are necessary?
Suggested answer
I would first define what “1,000 MW solar-plus-storage” means electrically: solar DC nameplate, inverter AC capability, maximum net export at the point of interconnection, battery charging and discharging power, and usable energy in MWh. I would confirm AC- or DC-coupling, shared equipment, collector-system losses, reactive capability, and the plant controller’s enforceable import and export limits. I would not assume that the battery eliminates solar export constraints or can provide unlimited-duration support.
I would coordinate the generation interconnection request and Full Interconnection Study scope with ERCOT and the lead transmission service provider. The operating matrix would include maximum permitted export, solar-only output, battery-only discharge, maximum grid charging, low-output voltage-control conditions, and credible transitions between modes. I would evaluate the proposed point of interconnection and reasonable alternatives across relevant study years, dispatches, load conditions, nearby resource additions, and transmission outages.
The technical work would include AC power flow and contingency analysis for thermal loading and voltages; reactive capability and voltage-control assessment; and short-circuit studies for breaker duty and protection coordination. Dynamic studies would examine fault ride-through, voltage recovery, frequency response, and plant-controller interactions. I would require suitable OEM models and use EMT analysis where control interactions, weak-system behavior, series compensation, or other risks justify it. Harmonic and resonance studies would address the converter and collector-system characteristics. I would also check the requirements applicable to the battery’s technology and interconnection-agreement date.
To establish upgrades, I would compare consistent cases with and without the project, identify its contribution to each limitation, and test mitigation alternatives. Options could include interconnection facilities, reconductoring, transformer or circuit additions, reactive equipment, controller changes, or a demonstrably enforceable operating limit. I would distinguish technical need from cost responsibility, which follows the governing process and agreements. My recommendation would state the supported operating envelope, upgrades, costs, schedule, and remaining congestion exposure. Approval to interconnect is not a promise of unrestricted output in every hour; I would separately evaluate curtailment risk and any applicable minimum-deliverability criteria.

 5.⁠ ⁠A hyperscale data center requests a 750 MW interconnection to the ERCOT grid with an aggressive energization schedule. How would you assess the technical feasibility, reliability impacts, and required transmission upgrades?
Suggested answer
I would begin by defining the actual grid demand and energization stages: whether 750 MW includes cooling and other auxiliary loads, hourly load shape, power factor, ramp rates, redundancy requirements, and the timing of each campus block. I would obtain the electrical configuration, UPS technology, backup-generation controls, and customer flexibility commitments. I would also confirm the project’s current ERCOT large-load pathway and eligibility with the interconnecting TSP. Under the current Section 9 framework, a request outside Batch Zero eligibility cannot receive initial energization approval until evaluated through a future study process. I would confirm the governing process before committing a date.
I would assess candidate connections using the relevant load forecasts, committed projects, and other large-load assumptions. AC contingency studies would identify corridor, transformer, and voltage constraints at each demand stage. Voltage-stability analysis would test reactive margins, while short-circuit and protection studies would check the proposed substation and affected system. I would evaluate maintenance and construction configurations, because a connection that works only with every facility available may not support the requested operating schedule.
The dynamic behavior is especially important. I would model the IT load, cooling motors, UPS converters, transfer logic, protection, and restoration sequence. I would assess faults, sudden loss of a large load block, simultaneous transfer to backup power, and reconnection ramps, including their effects on frequency and voltage. Generic models can support screening, but acceptance should rely on validated site-specific behavior. I would evaluate applicable Large Computational Load ride-through requirements, and use EMT and harmonic studies where fast controls or resonance could be material.
I would compare transmission reinforcements, reactive support, alternate connections, and a phased demand ramp. Any temporary import limit or controllable-load arrangement would require explicit approval, reliable measurement and enforcement, and demonstrated customer capability; backup generation would receive no assumed reliability credit without checking its operating constraints. I would present management with supported MW by stage, required upgrades, procurement and permitting dependencies, and schedule uncertainty. The aggressive date becomes a decision to manage through validated stages and clear readiness gates, rather than an assumption embedded in the study.

 6.⁠ ⁠With increasing penetration of inverter-based resources in ERCOT, what are the most critical dynamic stability challenges, and how would you evaluate potential solutions using PSS®️E, PSCAD, or TSAT?
Suggested answer
I would focus on four connected risks: frequency performance as synchronous inertia changes, voltage recovery and reactive-current limits, converter-control interactions in electrically weak areas, and widespread loss of inverter output during disturbances. Low system strength and low inertia are different problems and need different assessments. I would also examine subsynchronous or other poorly damped oscillations, interactions among plant controllers and nearby devices, and protection behavior with limited and controlled inverter fault current.
I would start with a credible network and validated resource models, including the intended firmware, protection settings, plant controller, current limits, and reactive-priority logic. In PSS®E or TSAT, I would screen broad system conditions and contingencies for frequency response, rotor-angle behavior of remaining synchronous machines, voltage recovery, and oscillatory performance. Cases would vary dispatch, synchronous-machine commitment, renewable output, topology, and battery operating point. I would use short-circuit-strength metrics as indicators, not as a universal pass/fail test or proof that EMT analysis is unnecessary.
For fast control interactions, unbalanced events, weak-network behavior, or suspected subsynchronous phenomena, I would build a PSCAD EMT study with an appropriately detailed surrounding network and verified OEM models. I would benchmark the initialized operating point and comparable responses against the positive-sequence study, investigate discrepancies, and check time-step and network-boundary sensitivity. Agreement between two simulations would increase confidence but would not replace validation against commissioning tests or measured disturbances. Missing model capabilities would be an explicit limitation.
I would match solutions to the demonstrated cause: coordinated control tuning, compliant ride-through settings, STATCOMs or synchronous condensers, transmission reinforcement, carefully validated grid-forming controls, or operating limits. Fast frequency response would be assessed with actual headroom, state of charge, response time, and sustainable energy. Grid-forming capability is not a blanket guarantee of stability. I would compare cost, implementation time, and effectiveness across the adverse cases, then define acceptance tests, monitoring, and change-control requirements so the as-built settings remain consistent with the studies.

 7.⁠ ⁠How would you compare two transmission expansion alternatives when one has a lower capital cost but higher congestion and operating costs, while the other requires a larger initial investment but provides greater long-term system benefits?
Suggested answer
I would first establish whether both alternatives satisfy the same reliability requirements, serve the same need, and can be delivered in time. An inexpensive option that fails a mandatory criterion is not a viable economic comparator. I would then establish a common study horizon, price basis, discount rate, load and generation assumptions, and treatment of residual value. Capital cost would include substations, protection, land, construction outages, and any dependent facilities, with the estimate uncertainty made visible.
I would compare the present value of capital, operating and maintenance costs, and future replacement or expansion needs. Chronological production-cost studies would quantify dispatch, fuel, startup, losses, and curtailment effects to the extent represented by the model. Consumer energy-cost savings would be evaluated separately from production-cost savings: these are different perspectives and cannot simply be added as independent benefits. I would similarly avoid counting a reduction in losses twice if it is already reflected in production costs.
For the higher-cost alternative, the decision is whether its incremental, non-overlapping benefits justify its incremental cost. I would calculate incremental net present value and the break-even annual benefit, then test low and high load growth, resource changes, fuel prices, construction delays, and cost overruns. I would also show benefits that are difficult to monetize, such as outage flexibility, resilience, or an expandable corridor, with clear evidence rather than an arbitrary dollar value.
For an economic-driven project seeking ERCOT endorsement, I would separately demonstrate the applicable formal economic test. ERCOT recognizes production-cost savings and congestion-cost savings through distinct tests; the latter evaluates system-wide consumer energy-cost reduction. A company's internal investment analysis does not replace those tests. I would recommend the alternative that meets the required need and offers the strongest defensible value across plausible futures, or a staged approach if it preserves value while limiting exposure to uncertain growth.

 8.⁠ ⁠Your engineering consultant delivers transmission planning results that differ significantly from ERCOT's published study findings. How would you identify the source of the discrepancies and validate the results?
Suggested answer
I would treat a large discrepancy as a reconciliation problem before deciding which result is correct. First, I would identify exactly what differs: the limiting contingency, overloaded equipment, voltage response, transfer limit, recommended upgrade, or economic benefit. I would confirm the ERCOT report's publication date, study year, scope, and assumptions. A public summary may omit details needed to reproduce a study, so I would obtain the authorized supporting cases and clarification through the appropriate channels.
I would create a side-by-side comparison of model versions, load allocation and power factor, generation status and dispatch, storage charging, planned-project inclusion, outages, branch ratings, transformer taps, shunt controls, reactive limits, and contingency definitions. For dynamic studies, I would also compare model libraries, load models, protection actions, fault location and clearing times, initialization, and integration settings. For economic studies, I would reconcile weather years, fuel prices, commitment constraints, transmission limits, outage treatment, and benefit calculations.
I would reproduce the consultant's baseline from the delivered inputs, then rerun the specific disputed cases using aligned assumptions. I would change one material input at a time and maintain a reconciliation table showing the resulting change. Checks would include power balance, equipment impedances and units, control limits, initialization quality, and whether reported violations survive appropriate solver diagnostics. A second tool or independent engineer can provide a useful check, but matching software outputs alone does not establish engineering validity.
Finally, I would classify the discrepancy as an input difference, legitimate scope difference, methodological issue, or error. I would hold decisions that depend on unresolved material findings, agree on corrective actions with the consultant and ERCOT as appropriate, and issue a documented reconciliation. If the available evidence cannot establish which result is reliable, I would state that limitation and identify the specific information or test needed to resolve it.

 9.⁠ ⁠What are the key differences between steady-state, transient stability, voltage stability, and short-circuit studies, and how would you determine which analyses are necessary for a proposed transmission project?
Suggested answer
Steady-state analysis evaluates an operating condition after the system has settled under the controls allowed for that study. AC power flow and contingency analysis show MW and Mvar flows, equipment loading, bus voltages, and reactive limits. They answer whether the network can carry the assumed dispatch and load under normal and specified outage conditions. They do not establish how the system behaves during the disturbance.
Transient stability analysis follows the time response to a disturbance such as a fault, line trip, or generation loss. It examines synchronous-machine angle behavior and the modeled response of inverter controls, voltage, frequency, and protection. Fault-clearing sequence, dynamic load behavior, controller limits, and validated models are essential. Depending on the phenomenon, I would use a positive-sequence dynamic simulation or a more detailed electromagnetic-transient study.
Voltage stability asks whether the system can maintain an acceptable voltage equilibrium and recover as loading, transfers, or disturbances increase reactive stress. P–V analysis assesses margin as transfer or load increases; Q–V analysis assesses reactive margin at selected buses. Dynamic studies are needed where motor recovery, load restoration, tap changers, or controls drive the outcome. A bus meeting its voltage limit in one power-flow solution does not, by itself, demonstrate adequate voltage-stability margin.
Short-circuit analysis determines fault-current levels and contributions for relevant fault types and network configurations. It supports breaker-duty checks and protection sensitivity and coordination. Grounding, sequence impedances, source strength, and inverter fault-current behavior must be represented appropriately. Both maximum duty and minimum fault current can matter; adding a line can increase fault duty even while improving thermal performance.
I would define the study scope from the project's electrical effects and applicable criteria. A transmission addition generally warrants steady-state and fault-duty screening; major transfer changes, weak-grid connections, large generation or load additions, or adverse screening results warrant deeper voltage and dynamic assessments. The final scope should be agreed with the responsible planning and protection teams, and successful performance in one study category would not substitute for the others.

Reference: PowerWorld voltage-stability analysis — P–V transfer analysis and Q–V reactive margins. Static voltage-stability methodology; dynamic phenomena require appropriate dynamic models.
Reference: PowerWorld transient-stability simulation — Disturbance events and fault initiation/clearing. Software methodology reference.

10.⁠ ⁠How would you evaluate transmission system performance under multiple future scenarios involving rapid load growth, renewable generation expansion, generation retirements, and transmission outages?
Suggested answer
I would build a small, decision-focused scenario matrix around the uncertainties most likely to change the investment decision: the location and timing of load growth, renewable additions, conventional-generation retirements, storage behavior, and transmission availability. I would distinguish the required planning cases from exploratory sensitivities. Each case would have a traceable source, effective study year, and rationale; a speculative project would not silently become a committed assumption.
I would preserve credible relationships between variables. For example, renewable output, weather-sensitive demand, and thermal-unit availability should reflect plausible coincident conditions, rather than independent extremes combined without justification. I would evaluate peak and relevant off-peak conditions, low renewable output with high demand, high export conditions, retirement delays or accelerations, and planned construction outages with the required additional contingencies. Those are proposed stress cases, not assumed ERCOT-mandated scenarios.
For each scenario, I would first establish a credible balanced dispatch and identify any supply inadequacy separately. I would then perform AC contingency analysis, followed by voltage, dynamic, short-circuit, and economic studies where the screen or project scope requires them. I would not add undocumented generation or discard nonconvergent cases merely to complete the matrix. Results would record binding constraints, severity, margins, curtailment, required upgrades, and the year each need appears.
I would compare alternatives on robustness across scenarios, identify common facilities, and quantify what changes the preferred choice. Probabilities would be used only where supported; otherwise I would present ranges, break-even conditions, and downside exposure. The recommendation might be a shared first stage with later additions triggered by verified load commitments or resource milestones. That gives management a build-now decision, an expansion path, and explicit conditions for revisiting the plan.

11.⁠ ⁠How would you identify and prioritize transmission investments that address both reliability violations and economic congestion within ERCOT?
Suggested answer
I would start with a map of demonstrated reliability needs and recurring or forecast congestion, linked to the affected facilities, contingencies, drivers, and required dates. Historical congestion is a screening signal; it is not sufficient economic justification by itself. I would verify whether a constraint is persistent, primarily outage-related, already addressed by a committed project, or likely to shift as load and generation change.
Mandatory reliability needs would set the minimum requirements and timing. I would then develop alternatives that address those needs while testing whether modest changes in scope could also reduce congestion, improve transfer capability, or avoid another future upgrade. Options might share a substation, right-of-way, or construction outage. I would coordinate with neighboring TSPs so that a local improvement does not move the problem elsewhere or duplicate another investment.
I would evaluate candidate portfolios, not simply add the benefits of isolated projects. Once one upgrade changes power flows, the incremental value of another may fall or rise. I would use consistent reliability cases and chronological economic simulations, apply the relevant ERCOT economic tests, and keep overlapping savings separate. Each candidate would have an explicit justification path: required reliability work, an economic enhancement, or another applicable planning basis.
For prioritization, I would consider the severity and timing of the reliability need, credible congestion savings, robustness to uncertainty, constructability, permitting and equipment lead times, and dependency on other projects. A weighted score can organize discussion, but it cannot offset a mandatory reliability deficiency. My recommendation would identify the first projects to advance, the assumptions supporting them, and the triggers for later stages, with a clear explanation of how the combined portfolio improves system performance and customer value.

16.⁠ ⁠How do you prioritize resources, manage competing deadlines, and maintain technical quality when overseeing several major transmission planning and interconnection projects simultaneously?

The lesson I carry into technical leadership is to test the input data and the analysis method independently, involve the responsible data owner, and make the correction traceable before relying on the study for a planning decision.

20.⁠ ⁠Imagine that ERCOT, a major customer, and your company's development team have conflicting priorities regarding the scope, cost, and schedule of a critical transmission project. How would you manage these competing interests and drive the project toward a successful outcome?
Suggested interview answer
I would first establish the decision that has to be made and the constraints each party is trying to protect. ERCOT and the transmission provider need acceptable system performance and an executable integration plan; the customer needs dependable capacity on a commercially useful schedule; and our development team needs a viable investment. Reliability criteria and required approvals would be fixed conditions, while the scope, sequencing, and commercial structure would be evaluated within those boundaries.
I would establish one agreed baseline for the requested capacity, load or generation profile, ramp-up, operating behavior, redundancy expectations, and target dates. Then I would ask the engineering team to produce a small set of credible options, potentially including a full build, phased capacity, or a different connection configuration. Each option would show the studies still needed, cost-estimate maturity, equipment lead times, construction outages, and external decisions on its critical path.
I would compare those options with the stakeholders using an explicit decision matrix and explain the practical tradeoffs. If an earlier date required reduced initial capacity, the customer should understand exactly what service could be supported and under what conditions. Any interim arrangement would need validated controls and the appropriate acceptance before becoming a commitment. Unresolved choices would be escalated to the responsible decision-makers with a recommendation and consequences for cost, schedule, and reliability.
Once a direction was agreed, I would document scope, ownership, milestones, assumptions, and change control. Regular technical and executive checkpoints would keep decisions timely. Success would mean the parties share an achievable plan and understand the conditions for delivery, with engineering evidence supporting the commitments made to the customer and the business.
